# Case Study: The Missing Parts

## Asterion Motion Systems' Material Shortage Problem

On a Thursday morning in July, Asterion Motion Systems' Chief Supply Chain Officer, Clara Neumann, walked past assembly line 3 and saw two versions of the same truth.

The planning system said that production for the next three days was feasible. The line supervisor pointed to an empty bin for a small sensor bracket and a quarantine pallet of motor controllers waiting for quality release. Purchasing reported that the supplier had confirmed delivery. Goods receipt showed a partial delivery. Production planning had already rescheduled the line twice that week.

Asterion was not facing a single supplier problem. It was facing several supply chain problems that happened to become visible as material shortages on the shop floor.

Electronic components had long lead times and frequent allocation changes. Machined metal parts depended on a small group of capacity-constrained suppliers. Standard components and fasteners were low value, but missing one of them could stop a high-value assembly. Some deliveries arrived late, some arrived incomplete, some arrived on time but were blocked in quality inspection, and some shortages seemed to result from outdated planning parameters rather than poor supplier behavior.

The executive board wanted a practical answer within four weeks. The company did not ask for a perfect academic model. It asked for a management recommendation that could be defended under questioning.

## Company Background

Asterion Motion Systems is a fictional German manufacturer of electromechanical drive modules used in industrial equipment, warehouse automation, and commercial vehicles. The company operates three plants and serves customers that expect stable delivery performance despite volatile demand and long supplier lead times.

The three material groups in scope are operationally distinct:

### Electronic Components

Electronic components include sensors, control boards, motor controllers, connectors, and semiconductor-based modules. Demand is linked to finished-goods orders, but supply is affected by long lead times, allocation, minimum order quantities, and engineering changes. Unit values are high and lead-time variability can be severe. A small delay can affect multiple production lines if the component is shared across products.

Electronic components are often single-sourced or dual-sourced with unequal supplier capability. Supplier confirmations are important, but not always reliable. Purchasing managers suspect that the planning system treats confirmed dates too optimistically.

### Machined Metal Parts

Machined metal parts include brackets, shafts, housings, gear components, and precision plates. Suppliers produce in batches and may face capacity constraints, tool availability problems, or quality deviations. Delays are often moderate but recurring. Quality holds can turn an apparently on-time delivery into an effective shortage.

Machined parts are replenished through purchase orders linked to production demand. Some suppliers are reliable for standard parts but unstable for tighter tolerances or short-notice schedule changes.

### Standard Components and Fasteners

Standard components and fasteners include screws, washers, pins, cable ties, small brackets, labels, packaging inserts, and other low-value parts. The purchasing value of a single item is low, but the operational impact of a stockout can be high. These parts are often managed with simpler planning parameters and less management attention.

Production teams argue that C-parts receive too little attention because their purchase value is small. Finance argues that increasing all safety stocks would tie up working capital without solving the real root cause.

## The Trigger

Asterion's production leadership observed that missed production slots had increased during the previous quarter. Purchasing argued that supplier markets had become more volatile and that many late deliveries were caused by external constraints. Production planning disagreed. It believed that supplier reliability was only part of the issue and that outdated safety stocks, unrealistic lead times, and weak shortage escalation were making the problem worse.

An internal audit selected six months of purchasing, goods receipt, production demand, shortage, and expedite records. The files include purchase orders, supplier confirmations, goods receipts, production requirements, shortage events, quality holds, premium freight records, supplier master data, material master data, plant data, and cost parameters. The data are not clean. They include missing confirmations, delayed postings, duplicate purchase order lines, partial receipts, inconsistent units of measure, lead-time outliers, and incomplete root-cause labels.

The board has asked an external student consulting team to answer one question:

> Where should Asterion intervene first if it wants to reduce material shortages and production disruptions without simply increasing inventory everywhere?

## The Decision

Clara Neumann must recommend a focused intervention program at the next executive meeting. She does not have the budget to dual-source every material, increase every safety stock, and expedite every critical part. She needs to know which supplier, material group, plant, planning parameter, or data-quality issue should be addressed first.

The student team must use the available data to build a defensible argument. A strong answer will not simply identify the supplier with the lowest on-time delivery rate. It will distinguish between supplier unreliability, material criticality, planning-parameter weakness, quality release problems, and data-quality noise.

## Management Constraints

Asterion's leadership has set five constraints:

1. Critical production lines may not be exposed to additional shortage risk without evidence.
2. Recommendations must be implementable in purchasing and production planning within eight weeks.
3. Any proposed KPI must be understandable to buyers, planners, and production managers.
4. The analysis must separate data-quality issues from supplier and planning performance where possible.
5. The final presentation must be concise enough for a 15-minute management meeting.

## Data Available

Students receive several Excel workbooks covering the manufacturing supply chain:

- Purchase order line records.
- Goods receipt transaction records.
- Production demand and material requirement records.
- Shortage event records.
- Expedite shipment and premium freight records.
- Supplier, material, plant, cost, and calendar reference tables.

Each split workbook contains:

- A main data sheet with operational records.
- Reference sheets with supplier, material, and plant attributes where useful.
- An assumptions sheet describing generation logic and intended teaching caveats.
- A split-info sheet where applicable.

The company warns that not every record should be taken at face value. The data reflect operational systems, not a cleaned analytical table.
