# Case Study: The Tender Trap

## Veridian Home & Living's Carrier Performance Problem

On a Monday morning in June, Veridian Home & Living's Chief Supply Chain Officer, Maya Stein, opened the weekly transport dashboard and saw a result that looked impossible.

Procurement had delivered exactly what the executive board had asked for: tendered freight rates were down, more lanes had multiple awarded carriers, and the negotiated cost per shipment looked better than the previous year. Yet the finance team reported rising spot freight spend. Sales complained about missed retail delivery appointments. The sustainability team warned that transport CO2 performance had moved in the wrong direction. Customer service had a growing list of late-delivery escalations.

The tender had saved money on paper. The network had become more expensive in practice.

The problem was not that cheap carriers were always bad. Some low-cost carriers performed well on short domestic lanes with stable demand. The problem was that Veridian had awarded too much volume to carriers whose low rates came with limited peak capacity, weaker acceptance behavior, older fleets, and poorer performance on complex cross-border lanes. When those carriers rejected tenders or missed appointments, planners used the spot market. The spot market solved the shipment, but often at a higher cost and with worse CO2 performance.

The executive board asked for a practical recommendation within four weeks. It did not ask for an academic model of every transport market. It asked for a management answer that could survive the CFO's question: "Are we sure the higher-rate carrier is actually cheaper once the whole system is considered?"

## Company Background

Veridian Home & Living is a fictional European producer and distributor of household appliances, home improvement products, and seasonal consumer goods. The company operates production plants and distribution centers in Germany, Poland, Italy, and the Benelux region. It ships full-truckload and less-than-truckload volumes to retailers, e-commerce fulfillment partners, wholesalers, and regional cross-docks.

The transport network is organized into lane groups:

### Domestic Core Lanes

Domestic core lanes connect Veridian's central warehouses with high-volume retail and wholesale customers. They have predictable weekly volumes, relatively short distances, and stable appointment structures. Tendered carriers can plan resources well, and low-cost carriers may be competitive if they have a dense local network.

On these lanes, the lowest rate is not automatically a bad decision. The operational question is whether the carrier has enough capacity during peak weeks and whether service remains stable when daily volume exceeds the forecast.

### Cross-Border Retail Lanes

Cross-border retail lanes connect production sites and distribution centers with large retail customers in France, Benelux, Austria, Switzerland, Italy, and Central Europe. These lanes have tighter appointment windows, more documentation, toll differences, and higher sensitivity to driver availability. A missed appointment can create a multi-day delay and customer escalation.

Low tender rates can hide risk if they depend on backhaul availability or subcontracted capacity. A carrier may be reliable in the tender presentation but unstable when the route requires short-notice capacity.

### Seasonal and Promotional Lanes

Seasonal and promotional lanes serve garden, cooling, heating, and holiday-related assortments. Demand spikes around promotional windows and weather-driven events. Tendered weekly capacity may be sufficient on average but insufficient in peak weeks.

Spot freight is common on these lanes. The analytical challenge is to decide whether spot freight reflects poor carrier performance, poor demand planning, or a deliberate economic choice.

### Sustainability-Critical Lanes

Sustainability-critical lanes are long-distance lanes where intermodal rail-road options, alternative fuels, or high load factors can materially reduce CO2 emissions. The sustainability team wants more volume on lower-emission carriers and modes. Transport procurement worries that these options cost more and may reduce flexibility.

The relevant question is not simply whether emissions can be reduced. It is where emissions can be reduced without creating unacceptable service or cost penalties.

## The Trigger

In the annual transport tender for 2026, Veridian changed its award logic. Historically, carrier allocation combined price, service history, capacity, and sustainability. Under cost pressure, procurement increased the weight of price and shifted larger primary shares to the lowest-rate bidder on many lanes.

For the first two months, reported tender savings looked attractive. By May, however, operational symptoms accumulated:

- Tender rejection rates increased on several cross-border and promotional lanes.
- Spot freight costs rose, especially during peak weeks and near month-end.
- Missed retail appointments increased for large customers with strict unloading windows.
- CO2 per shipment rose on lanes where older road fleets replaced intermodal or higher-load-factor carriers.
- Claims and customer escalations increased, but the cost was not always linked back to the tender decision.

The CFO argued that higher spot cost might simply reflect market volatility. Procurement argued that carriers could not be punished for demand spikes outside the forecast. Sales argued that a low freight rate is irrelevant if a shipment misses a retail promotion window. Sustainability argued that transport procurement had optimized the wrong scorecard.

Maya Stein asked an external student consulting team to answer one question:

> Which carrier allocation and tender governance changes would reduce true transport cost while protecting service and CO2 performance?

## The Decision

Veridian must decide how to prepare the next mini-tender. Management can adjust awarded shares, renegotiate capacity commitments, change penalty clauses, use different mode choices on sustainability-critical lanes, and introduce a new carrier scorecard. It cannot replace every carrier immediately, and it cannot pay premium rates on every lane.

The student team must use the available data to build a defensible argument. A strong answer will not simply rank carriers by tender rate or on-time delivery. It will distinguish between contracted price, actual paid cost, spot substitution, service failure cost, customer impact, CO2 target deviation, and data-quality noise.

## Management Constraints

Veridian's leadership has set five constraints:

1. Strategic retail customers may not be exposed to additional service risk without evidence.
2. Recommendations must be implementable before the next mini-tender cycle.
3. Any proposed KPI must be understandable to procurement, transport planning, finance, sales, and sustainability.
4. The analysis must separate data-quality issues from carrier and lane performance where possible.
5. The final presentation must be concise enough for a 15-minute management meeting.

## Data Available

Students receive several Excel workbooks covering the transport tendering process and shipment execution:

- Shipment execution records.
- Spot-market quote records.
- Tender bid records.
- Lane award and contracted allocation records.
- Weekly carrier scorecards.
- Claims and service-failure records.
- Carrier, lane, site, customer, calendar, cost, and emissions reference tables.

Each split workbook contains:

- A main data sheet with operational records.
- Reference sheets with carrier, lane, and site attributes where useful.
- An assumptions sheet describing generation logic and intended teaching caveats.
- A split-info sheet where applicable.

The company warns that not every record should be taken at face value. The data reflect operational systems, tender files, transport planning tools, and carrier scorecards. They do not form a cleaned analytical table.
